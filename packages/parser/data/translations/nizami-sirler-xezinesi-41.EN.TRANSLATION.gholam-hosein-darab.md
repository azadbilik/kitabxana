A Magian from the empire of India walked to a flower garden.

He saw a resort like a beautiful picture; he found a kingdom decorated with unreality.

The rose bud, like heaven, had girt its loins for bloodshed; the short-lived tulip was unaware of itself.

In the meadow grew flowers of many colours. Much wine had been mixed with sugar.

The rose, like a shield, was wounded by its own arrow; the willow tree trembled for fear of its own life.

The tresses of the violet were the chain on its own neck; the eyes of the narcissus were the silver coins in its lap.

The tulip had ground gems and the rose turquoises, the tulip which lasts for one breath and the rose for one day.

Their respite was not for more than an instant; yet none thought of the future.

The sage passed through that beautiful garden, and after some months he again passed that way.

Instead of those roses and nightingales which he had seen in the garden, he heard only the cries of kites and crows.

Hell had displaced paradise; the Cæsar of that palace had removed to a hovel.

The verdure had rotted; the bouquets of roses had become heaps of thorns.

The sage contemplated that scene intently; he laughed at all and wept for himself.

He said: “Though it has a time of outward appearance, nothing is meant to be permanent.

“The end of all things, produced by dust and water, is destruction.

“There is no better street than the street of destruction; there is no other way for me than the way of destruction.”

When the vision of grace increased his sight, he knew himself and knew God.

He became the jeweller of the gem of that secret, until at his death he returned to essence.

Oh thou, who art a Mosalmān and not yet as good as a Magian, thou art a spring and hast not a drop of water.

Be not less than that Magian of India; abandon the world and say to it: “Be not.”

How long wilt thou hold up thy head impertinently like the rose and pride thyself on thy crown and thy belt?

Rise and unloose the belt of clay, because the world has girt its loins for thy blood.

The crown and the belt are the calamities of love, pledge them both to the tavern of love.

Sometimes thy crown makes thee the master of the earth; sometimes thy belt gives thee the servitude of the heart.

Strive to free thyself from this duality of master and servant; or, like Nezāmi, free thyself from thyself.
