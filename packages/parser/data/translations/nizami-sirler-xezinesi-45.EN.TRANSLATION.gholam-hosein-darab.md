A pilgrim started on his pilgrimage; he practised the customs of the pilgrims.

All that he had above his requirements, amounted to one purse of dinārs.

He said: “My heart tells me that a certain noble Sufi who has renounced the world,

“Is a religious man; if others are untrustworthy, he is to be trusted.”

He went out and brought him in secretly to his own house and entrusted him with his purse of dinārs.

He said: “Guard this secretly; then bring it back to me when I return.”

The Hāji started on his way to the desert; the old man took away the gold which was entrusted to him.

Oh Lord, I take refuge in thee! How long was it that the heart of the poor man yearned for that gold?

He said: “I will straighten out my affairs with the gold; I have found the treasure which I required.

“I will use it up quickly, that I may not be tied to that which God gives so secretly.”

He untied the knot of that string and enjoyed happiness for some nights.

He spent on his belly all that gold with which he was entrusted, and he worshipped his belly.

He laid his hand on that casket of dinārs; he made the tresses of the beauties rings on his girdle.

He tore the pious robe of the Sufi; all that was left to him was grief and many excuses.

He ate the prey, till not even a sign of it was left, and not even tallow remained for his lamp.

When our pilgrim returned from his journey, he paid an unexpected visit to the dishonest man.

He said: “Oh sage, bring it to me.” He answered: “What?” He said: “The gold.” The Sufi replied: “Be silent,

“Be generous, and do not dispute. Who can take revenue from a ruined village?

“The purse was spent on lust and desire; there is a great distance between a poor man and a purse.

“No one has carried off booty from a Turk; no man has ever entrusted his belongings to a rogue.

“Your gold broke the pillar of my heart, and I was broken by the dust which settled upon me.”

With a hundred smiles he spent the money; with a hundred tears he stood before him.

He said: “Be generous, because we repent; we were infidels and have become Mosalmāns.

“Nature is pregnant with vice; if there is vice, the fault is mine.”

Until the generosity of God said to him sternly: “Arise, because the dervish is standing, arise.”

When the owner of the silver turned to God, he was generous and let the matter drop.

He said to himself: “Do not be hard on him; he has nothing. What can I take from nothing?

“What can I take from him who has not a grain of barley and has no pledge other than pleas for forgiveness?

“That which remains to the Sufi of that property, is a round Mim and a Kufic Alef.”

He said: “If thou dost not wish me to ill-treat thee, and that I should make lawful to thee that which is unlawful,

“Oh thou, who art as deceitful as the heaven, do not have a short sleeve and a long hand.”

There is no heart which is free from greed and envy; none is to be trusted on this earth.

Religion is good money, do not entrust it to the devil; do not give the royal armlet to the keeper of dogs.

If thou shouldst give it, oh master, the loss is thine; none can take back capital from the poor.

This is a stage of error, take virtue as thy provision. Lay hold on the skirts of religion and retire.

Heaven does not rob the poor; it robs the caravans of the rich.

Since the guardian of this road is a robber, poverty is better than wealth.

By experience, I found that the bee stings him who desires the honey.

The flesh of the lion is bitter, so that after its death it may not be eaten by wild animals.

The candle burns out through standing upright. The moon wanes through seeking perfection.

The wind which is both at war and at peace with the dust, is safe on this road through its poverty.

Does the seagull know that the calamity of the fish is its silver?

When gold is the scale of thy need, it becomes the opening chapter of thy five prayers.

Thou wilt not be free from the road of this need, until like Nezāmi thy life is pure.
