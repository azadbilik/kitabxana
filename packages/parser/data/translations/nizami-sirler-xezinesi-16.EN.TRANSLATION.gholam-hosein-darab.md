When my trainer began to break me in, he freed me from the knots of the nine spheres of heaven.

Though the rope was full of knots, he did not give up the struggle to untie them,

Until this affair reached a stage where the knots in the rope were untied.

Briefly, the master who is in our prison, although he is not God, yet he is our Lord.

He is my guide in both worlds; otherwise why should he be anxious about my soul?

Though I have not often been in accord with him, yet he will not withhold his affection from me.

That trainer tamed me, when he became a slave to me who was untamed.

He was not defeated by such as me. He considered association with dust as a gain.

One day, from this Egypt which shelters Potiphar’s wife, Joseph-like he came out of the well.

When the eyes of night closed in sleep, they lit the eye of the lamp of dawn.

The dawn became a lamp, illuminating the sky; the black cloak of night turned into the red border of the day.

The master took a lamp, held my hand, and led me to a garden.

He freed my skirt from the thorn of grief, when he filled my lap with roses.

I laughed like the lips of the tulip and tore my garment in a hundred places like the rose.

The tulip entrusted its own heart to my soul; the rose girded my loins with its own belt.

At times I blushed all over like one drunk with wine; at other times I blossomed out like a rose.

I hurried from rose to rose and from branch to branch to where the water was gushing forth,

Until the banner of love reached a place where a perfume of fidelity scented the air.

A subtle breeze with an eloquent tongue, like the breath of Messiah, made my heart live.

It cast down my litter and gave my steed to the wind.

It said to me: “Dismount and do not speak of thyself; otherwise I will separate thee from thyself.”

I, who was like a boat on that water, was calmed by that heavenly breeze.

The water was flowing, and I dismounted; parched with thirst, I reached the bank of the river.

It was a spring brighter than the sun; the Prophet Khezr had not seen it even in his dreams in heaven.

Its jasmine garden was a resting place; its open narcissus would lull one to sleep.

It was on the circle of heaven; the ambergris of the perfume of Paradise was its slave.

The rose had a place on the bosom of the jasmine; the thorn trod on the skirt of the rose.

The gazelle and the fox had given musk to the rose and fur to the thorn in that meadow.

The parrot had shed its feathers on the grass because of that sweetly smelling rose.

The green herbs were as beautiful as the plumage of parrots. The gazelles were fattened by their sweetness.

The north wind blew through the bower of roses. The gazelles ate flowers from the branches of the herbs.

The variegated daisies were massed together and fanned the air with grey ambergris.

Its scene gave collyrium like the narcissus. Its vegetation was tiger lilies like emerald.

The jasmine and the rose robbed the caravan. The turtle dove and the nightingale sang together.

The day-lily with the tongue of Jesus showed the hand of Moses to the dawn.

The ring dove, crying in the dawn, made the heavens grey with its sighs.

The breeze was writing the story of the rose upon the leaves of the palm willow with a hand of hope.

At times the spring came to greet the meadow; at times the rose came in thanksgiving to the thorn.

The white jasmine had pitched its tent on the plain; the pennon on the tent pole reached to the Pleiades.

The tulip came to the fire temple of mystery and prayed like the Indian Magi.

The black tulip and the white jasmine were like the crow and the Canopus Star of Yaman.

The boat of the garden, with red and yellow banners, had windows of lapiz lazuli.

The water was smooth like ermine; how beautiful is ermine with sable!

The branches were stirred by the light of heaven and scattered silver coins beneath the feet of the shadows.

The shadows spoke with their lips of sunshine. The pebbles were alive swimming in the water.

The white rose was blistered by the kisses of the hyacinth; the lips of the rose were chafed by the eyelashes of the bud.

As the quiver of the dandelion was empty of arrows, sometimes it asked for a shield and sometimes for quarter.

The willow, struck by magic, trembled; the tulip was the censer for its incense.

The meadow was so full of life that it wished to fly; the jasmine was so delicate that it wished to drop.

The sugar cane had sprouted with sweet laughter; the brown rose bush had come forth with red shoes.

The self-willed wild rose spoke by the breath of the wind.

The sky was greener than the leaf of the tangerine tree and had appeared at morning with an orange in its hand.

Since the sky had decorated its banner in that place, the meadow demanded its boat.

Every knot on the string of that green table-cloth was the life of the earth and the heart of the sky.

It seemed as though the star of good omen said to the earth in the morning: “Mayest thou be happy”,

Or that the sky had passed by and exchanged its emeralds for rubies.

The spring was brighter than the eyes of the celestial maidens, that it might dim the source of the light of the sun.

The verdure had made its ablutions at that spring, gave thanks for its purification, and arose.

The bird had inhaled the perfume of Solomon from the rose; therefore it sang the songs of David.

The claws of the partridge, stained with the blood of the pheasant, hung a chain around the bole of the cypress tree.

The law-givers of the garden ordained for the nightingale the death of the crow.

The secret of the heart of the owl whose body was the product of that land, was his own death sentence.

The north wind, with the help of radiant Canopus, tanned the rough leather of the earth to smoothness.

The tulip was breathless with the palpitation of its heart, because of the speed with which it had hastened.

The shadow of the beauty-loving box tree had laid its hand on the heart of the tulip.

The silvery fingers of the dawn-coloured jasmine had taken from the night the whole of its crescent moon.

The Joseph-like sun, roped with gold, dug the pit of the dimple of the jasmine.

The earth had put on a robe of yellow muslin like the Jews, because the water showed its white hand like Moses.

The earth had made a medicine of that water, and whatever it had swallowed it brought forth again.

The light of the dawn had found the arena free. The branches gave leave to the breeze to walk in the shade.

The shadows bit the lips of the sun, and the wind combed the tresses of the willow.

Shadow and sunshine danced on the banks of the stream through the banners of the branches.

The thorn became aloe-wood which was my object, and the fire of the rose was the censer for that incense.

The stalk of the rose was the pulpit of the nightingale, and the tendrils of the violet were the girdle of the rose.

The bird sang more sweetly than David; the rose scattered more nectar than Nezāmi.
